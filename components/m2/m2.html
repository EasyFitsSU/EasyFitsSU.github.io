Problem identification

Initially, we wanted to find a simple issue that almost everybody deals with and that can be solved. After thinking of various problems that people face on a daily basis, we came to the conclusion that outfit planning and getting dressed can easily become a time-consuming activity, whether we're talking about people who have no particular interest in fashion, or those who are passionate about it. Getting dressed can become particularly frustrating whenever we are testing outfits, especially when we cannot find the styling that we like. It goes hand in hand with outfit planning: painting an image of an outfit in our minds just to try it on and realise that we don't like it. Besides, there's a big chance that our wardrobes are filled with clothes that we rarely wear or ones that we forgot about. Therefore, almost everyone deals with the questions "How would this look on me?", "How would those clothes look in an outfit?" and "What goes well with this?".

Initial solution

After identifying the potential problems and exploring possible solutions, we came up with EasyFits, a 3D digital wardrobe designed to simplify outfit planning and clothing management.

The platform's main distinguishing feature is a 3D model based on the user's body silhouette, which acts as a virtual mannequin. Users can visualize different clothing items and complete outfits without having to try them on physically, helping them better understand how different combinations might look on them.

EasyFits aims to rely on machine learning and computer vision rather than generative AI to process the user's silhouette and clothing items. Additionally, the platform provides outfit recommendations based on the user's virtual wardrobe. For example, it could suggest combining grey trousers with a burgundy sweater that the user may have forgotten about, helping them discover new combinations using clothes they already own.

Customer discovery plan

For the customer discovery step, we wanted to validate whether the problems we identified in relation to outfit planning and clothing management are experienced by our potential customers, how frequently and how big of an impact they have on their daily lives. Our initial hypotheses are that people may experience difficulties when choosing outfits, combining clothing items they already own, keeping track of their wardrobe, and deciding whether to purchase clothing online. We also want to understand how people currently deal with these situations and whether existing methods or applications adequately address their needs.

To investigate these hypotheses, we used two different research methods:
1. Online questionnaire: we distributed a Google Forms questionnaire to collect responses from a broader group of potential customers. The questions focus on clothing habits, wardrobe organization, outfit selection and online shopping experiences. The results help with identifying recurring behaviors and patterns, as well as potential problem areas.
2. Face-to-face interviews: We conducted two semi-structured interviews with selected participants to gain a deeper understanding of their experience. The questions are asked in a way that aims for the participants to describe specific situations they encountered, without inducing the idea that there should be, in fact, a problem.

Key notes

What the data supports

  • 89% already organise their wardrobe in some way (48% by category, 36% by season). People who care about order would see a clear benefit in organising their clothes through our app, and the 11% who don't organise at all get a structure with almost no effort.
  • 83% found a forgotten piece of clothing in the last year. Keeping everything in one digital database can lower this number and make people more aware of what they own. It also gives the outfit recommendations something to work with, like resurfacing a forgotten sweater in a new combination.
  • 81% plan their outfit ahead at least sometimes (33% often or always). People already plan, so they would use the app for planning, and doing it digitally should be faster than imagining the outfit or pulling clothes out of the closet.
  • 48% need more than 5 minutes to decide what to wear, and 45% try on three or more outfits. Virtual try-on lets them test combinations without changing clothes, which means they can try more outfits and decide faster.
  • 54% find styling easy (1-2 out of 5), and 46% rate it 3 or harder. People who are good at combining clothes can put that skill to use in the app, and those who struggle get recommendations and a way to preview outfits.

Where we need to be careful

  • Not everyone sees getting dressed as a problem. 88% decide what to wear in under 10 minutes and 54% find styling easy, so the outfit-recommendation side will matter mostly to the minority who struggle. The strongest evidence for the digital wardrobe is the forgotten clothes (83%), not the time spent choosing.
  • Most people already have a system, so the app has to be easier than what they do now. 89% organise their clothes in some way, and building a digital wardrobe takes effort, because every item has to be added. How willing people are to do that, and what would put them off, is covered in the analysis of opinions on the app below.

Key notes

What the data supports

  • 56% buy mostly or only in stores, against 20% who buy mostly or only online. Most people still prefer to see and try clothes physically, which shows how many doubts remain around online shopping. A realistic 3D try-on can reduce them and bring more of these people online.
  • 89% ask themselves "do I have anything to match it with?" at least sometimes, and 64% do it often or always. The question comes up with almost every purchase. The app can answer it by showing a new piece next to the clothes they already own.
  • 37% have bought an almost identical piece without realising. Most people (57%) haven't, so this is a smaller problem than the others, but the digital wardrobe prevents it for free, since they can check what they own before buying.
  • Among the 50 people who buy online, 48% returned an order in the last 6 months and 40% have ordered the same item in two sizes to return one. Together, 60% did at least one of the two. Online shopping often ends in sending clothes back, because people can't tell how an item will fit. A try-on on their own body model targets exactly that.
  • Enjoyment of trying on clothes in stores is split: 37% don't enjoy it, 35% do, and 29% are neutral. For the people who don't enjoy it, virtual try-on can cut the time they spend in fitting rooms by narrowing down what to try physically.
  • The reasons people postpone online orders are the problems we set out to solve. 72% have size doubts, 66% don't know how the item will look on their body, and 13% don't know how it will go with their clothes. In total, 88% selected at least one of these three.
  • Returns come from fit and look. Of the people who returned something, 77% said the size didn't fit and 41% said it looked different on them than in the photos, and 90% gave at least one of these two reasons. A 3D model of their own body addresses both.

Where we need to be careful

  • A 3D body model does not solve every online-shopping doubt. 42% hesitate because the material differs from the photos and 41% of the returns were for quality. The try-on addresses size, fit and look, but fabric quality will need extra work, such as detailed material information.
  • Interest in trying clothes in stores is real, and the store side is untested. 56% prefer buying in stores and 35% enjoy trying clothes on, so online try-on will not replace the store for everyone. The form only asked customers, so whether stores will scan their items and pay for the service still needs to be validated.

Key notes

What the data supports

  • 67% say a 3D view would make them more likely to buy clothes online more often (4 or 5 out of 5), and only 19% say it would not. This fits the doubts from the previous section: the 72% unsure about size and the 66% unsure how an item looks on their body are what the 3D model is meant to answer.
  • Virtual try-on is the feature people value most. 63% picked try-on with clothes from online stores, 58% try-on with the clothes they own, and 53% outfit suggestions. Both halves of the idea, the wardrobe and the try-on before buying, are wanted, and people are less interested in a 360° view (28%).
  • Most people are willing to scan a good part of their wardrobe. 64% would scan more than a quarter of their clothes, 38% more than half and 23% more than three quarters. That is enough to start with if the first items they add already give them something useful.

Where we need to be careful

  • Scanning is the biggest barrier. 61% say scan time would discourage them and 41% say privacy. 36% would scan less than a quarter of their wardrobe, 11% of them none, and 11% say they would use the app only once. Onboarding has to be quick, we should make clear how body scans are stored, and the app should be useful even when only part of the wardrobe is scanned.
  • Organising the wardrobe is not what attracts people. Wardrobe organisation was the least picked feature (23%), even though 89% already organise their clothes. People want what the app does with the clothes (try-on, outfits) more than a place to store them, so the digital wardrobe is better presented as the base for those features than as the main selling point.
  • Willingness to pay by users is low. 66% want a free plan with limited features, 23% a one-time payment and only 11% a monthly or yearly subscription. Our main revenue will therefore come from the stores that scan their items, which this form did not test. We will also need a plan that gives the 34% who are willing to pay a clearly better experience, while users on the free plan bring in revenue through ads and premium features.
  • These are intentions, not behaviour. The 67% answered a hypothetical question, and 19% said a 3D view would not make them buy more often. The real interest will only be visible once people try a prototype.

Key notes

What the data supports

  • Almost nobody has used an app like this. Of the 45 people who answered this open question, 44 had never used a wardrobe or outfit app. There is little existing habit in this audience and no dominant product to displace, so the space is still largely unexplored.
  • Nobody mentioned an app that simulates trying on clothes. The try-on side, which respondents rated as the most useful feature, was not covered by anything they had used.

Where we need to be careful

  • "Unexplored" is not the same as "no competition". One respondent already uses a wardrobe app (Whering) that digitises clothes from photos and suggests combinations, so the basic digital wardrobe is not new. Our difference has to be the 3D body model and the try-on with store clothes.
  • Few users can also mean low awareness or low need. People may not look for this kind of app because they don't know it exists, or because they don't feel they need one. We will have to show quickly what the app gives them.
  • This is one open question answered by 45 people, and 19 left it blank, so it shows direction rather than a full market picture. A proper competitor analysis would give a stronger view.

Interview 1: group interview

There are three participants (Andra, Simona and Sebi) and the interview is about 12 minutes long. The questions asked were neutral and focused on habits, not on a specific problem.

Andra

  • Plans her outfit the evening before, using Pinterest for inspiration
  • Doesn't always like Pinterest outfits or feel like wearing them
  • Picks what suits her and prefers simple clothes
  • Keeps her wardrobe sorted by type of item
  • Struggles to pair a dress with the shoes she owns
  • Sometimes repeats outfits, so she recombines her pieces
  • Shopped online once (gym clothes) and is skeptical of listed measurements
  • Has never used a 3D wardrobe app
  • Faces the problem we address? Partly: she struggles to pair clothes and repeats outfits, but plans calmly

Simona

  • Plans her outfit about 10 minutes before and often changes her mind
  • Chooses comfort first and design second, and wants practical pieces
  • Finds trying on several items time-consuming
  • Keeps her wardrobe sorted by type of item
  • Finds it hard to pair a T-shirt for a formal event
  • Reinvents outfits by swapping one everyday item for one that she doesn't wear as often
  • Hasn't ordered clothes online
  • Has never used a 3D wardrobe app
  • Faces the problem we address? Yes: changing her mind, trying on several items and formal pairings all take her time or effort

Sebi

  • Builds his outfit around his pants, in about 5 minutes
  • Plans formal outfits days ahead and avoids repeating outfits close together
  • Dislikes shopping and asks someone else's opinion before buying
  • Keeps his wardrobe sorted by type of item
  • Owns clothes he doesn't know how to pair and hasn't tried reinventing outfits
  • Hasn't ordered clothes online for himself
  • Has never used a 3D wardrobe app
  • Faces the problem we address? Yes: he owns clothes he can't pair and relies on other people's opinions

Key takeaways

  • Virtual try-on with the 3D model could save time for people like Simona, who finds trying on several items slow.
  • All three struggle to pair some clothes and only see their wardrobe by item, so an outfit builder and "what goes with this?" suggestions are useful.
  • Andra repeats outfits and Sebi avoids repeating them, so an outfit planner that tracks recently worn outfits would help.
  • They do little to no online clothes shopping, and Andra is skeptical of listed measurements, so a 3D model of the user's own body could help.

Only three participants in one session, so these are indications, not proof.
